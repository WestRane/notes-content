## Triumph of Archaic Wrapping and Protracted Catharsis
*September 2026*

It's been a long way since Resident Evil series started getting sort of a reincarnation. Capcom introduced their RE Engine back in 2017 and had the seventh game released with this engine as a backbone, returning to the creepy atmosphere and first-person perspective.

I was never introduced to this series. I never played it, never watched it, and I just knew it's scary and there were zombies. From that point of view, Resident Evil 2 Remake seems like a really good starting point, as it features the same engine, good optimization, visuals and sound design, and more than twenty years of cumulative experience to develop and write these stories. The game is indeed very popular, so nothing could go wrong even if I'm not a fan of horrors, right? Right?

### Maze Brilliance and Dreariness of Repetition

What can an average game offer to a player when it comes to location design? And we're not talking about possible visuals, we're thinking of blueprints of places where the story takes place.

Single-player games do tend to either show off a big and vibrant open world or create a linear story with multiple small corridor-ish locations that replace each other throughout the story. Resident Evil 2... well, it obviously doesn't take the first path, given the release date of the original game, but it doesn't exactly strike me as the second either. While it plainly swaps RPD with the sewers and then with Umbrella's labs, the initial gameplay in the first location heavily relies on noticeable backtrack-heavy looping and the scene-setting is obviously claustrophobic.

The usage of the very same corridors over and over again was genuinely practical back in 1998. Not a single gamer had a PC capable of running a carefully crafted zombie-inhabited police station in full 3D, so it was a calculated decision to use Metroidvania style for the locations. They locked players inside of one building and forced them to obtain more and more lore knowledge, tools and powers to actually open every single door and get the full view of the game story.

This decision holds up to this day, especially when running from a crowd of zombies to a new door, unlocking it with a new key you grabbed recently and then you're suddenly in a familiar corridor. Even the zombies in that well-trodden corridor are somehow familiar as well. This happens all the time, and I would lie if I said it doesn't feel good. But at the same time this means we have to go through this corridor again. And again. And again.

Most of the gamers aren't exactly struggling to render an open-world game in 2019, right? So this is no longer the issue at all, but then the question arises as to what's the reason to make a game with such a repetitive location design?

1. RE2 is a remake. This reason is the most obvious one, because the developers are clearly targeting those who have played the original game. We do get pieces of the story now and then, but it's up to actual series' fans to feel the nostalgia with these corridors again, now in 3D, with new visuals and sound design and many other game strengths. This wouldn't be a remake if the authors changed the layout and the genre, as this would be a somewhat RE-inspired game then. So they had to create the exact same structure.
2. It makes the locations feel like puzzle boxes. You need to get to the west wing of the building? Sure, no problem, but you have to head to the east wing first and solve just a few tasks there. After you do that, you can go to the west wing, only to find out you have to go back to the east wing just a few moments later. You end up being thrown between rooms, floors and wings. It's a hellish cycle that genuinely feels great, because the more you discover, the more you feel like you're structuring an entire map of the location in your head. The more you play, the more shortcuts you obtain, therefore having even more access to planning how you move around the place.

But it also inevitably comes with its deep archaic flaws. You have to endure the same surroundings multiple times. This isn't exactly a big problem when searching for chess plugs in the sewers, because the player mostly only visits rooms maybe 2 to 4 times in total. Moreover, some rooms like storage ones only require the player to visit them once, grabbing the pieces and happily never coming back there. It feels incredibly balanced and interesting.

As for the Raccoon Police Department... it's notoriously distinct. The corridors are genuinely scary and very atmospheric, but this odd pleasure of seeing the scary immersive mix vanishes after wandering around for maybe an hour or two. As the time goes on, the roaming starts to feel like writing a dissertation. You re-read all your text over and over again, searching for new typos and new places to insert some additional info, and it becomes incredibly frustrating after a short while.

Same goes for RPD. It's not scary to see the very same zombie near the very same window in the same dark corridor for the fifth time. And it's not much more engaging to see a new licker in this corridor after investing some time into progressing. This is still just a police station corridor, with average walls, stickers, papers and mess. For some players this complicated, overly intertwined number of rooms *is* the game, but for me it stops being one much sooner than I wish it did. Moreover, it creates a deep ludonarrative dissonance, when Leon - a cop on his first day - goes and slowly, leisurely cracks riddles instead of actively caring about Claire and Martin and the world.

This feels absolutely in place when thinking about the old games, as many of them used this trick. Moreover, Resident Evil is definitely a "genre-defining" one (as they state in the description) among the other classic examples. But it doesn't mean it is still interesting to see this in a game released in 2019. The maze isn't beautiful when you spend 5+ hours inside of it. It's grueling, because it feels like Capcom released a gorgeous 3D game with the skeleton of a 90s point-and-click adventure.

### How Mr. X shakes things up

At some point there's no longer pleasure in running - or rather walking slowly - around the police station. It's become a homework, and the player is forced to endless encounters with endless zombies and the game never lets you feel the vibe of "okay I killed a few zombies here, so this room will be much safer from now on". It just doesn't give you enough ammo to do that to the entire station, and that's completely reasonable, given the genre. This means you're **always** surrounded by zombies, even at the main hall at times.

And yet, it's ironic to feel that the department building starts to feel somewhat safer as the time goes on. Yeah, the cost of it is walking to the same rooms and using conceptually the same items, but then the game... shakes things up a little. When I encountered Mr. X at first, I thought to myself: oh, wow, the second boss of the game and I have many shotgun shells, this is going to be intense and immersive. And I tried to kill him. He fell to his knee and Leon, catching his breath slightly, noted we should run away. I frowned deeply at that, but having the developers saying this, I just couldn't go against them, so I ran. Only to find out he can hear me almost anywhere when I'm running.

The monster, gentleman-ish and horrific as he was, started evoking complicated feelings in me more than just a mountain of terror after some time. The game wants you to solve mysteries and wander around the station searching for new keys, doors, safes. These quests aren't exactly very IQ-demanding, and we'll talk about that later, but they still require some amount of investment into thinking what's going on and what we need. When there's a giant invincible monster who will always be able to eventually find you... well, it doesn't make the game better.

His footsteps stopped sending shivers down my spine, and instead became a trigger for endless sighs and groans slipping off my tongue. He builds a great vibe of panic, horror. Moreover, he forces the player to use his cumulative location knowledge to get rid of the monster for some time. But here's the catch: you were obligated to walk the long paths only to be able to open more shortcuts, and now developers intentionally force-feed you with walking in long circles **again** just to run away from Tyrant. It doesn't feel rewarding by any means. I remember playing the original Syberia game back on DVD-player, and there were multiple mysteries there too, but it felt contemplative because nothing was trying to kill me while I thought. While Resident Evil 2 layers panic onto the same structure, these two logics fight.

Contrary to how you'd expect a horrific creature to be killed or at least let you breathe for a while, it hears any sound and therefore comes to you immediately, abruptly stopping you from searching rooms, moving shelves, killing zombies (and there are even more lickers and zombies at the time) and simply playing the game. It's not a mini-game with fighting the boss, it's a disaster mid investigation which doesn't allow you to play **at all**. You have to AFK for a while for it to go away, then you do something you're required to do, then he comes back and you're forced to either run in terror or hide and AFK again, having nothing else to do elsewhere because the game is pretty linear. A potential strength became one of the deepest, tedious flaws of the entire game.

### Character Depths and Structural Gaps

#### Leon and Claire

Despite the banners of the game where both Leon and Claire stand close to each other as if covering each other's side, the actual gameplay and cutscenes are completely different. We don't see Claire at all, except for some small brief cutscenes once a few hours. She is somewhere nearby, but we can't meet her. It holds up well in terms of survival horror immersion and tension, but it's a deceptive outcome nonetheless.

The game lets us start with a notable motivation - let's get out of here together. We're forced to split, which is fine regarding the genre, but did this motivation stand out as something really worth even mentioning? Absolutely not. The game in the Leon part doesn't care about Claire at all. I genuinely think it's a very controversial decision, as it literally kills a potentially good character-driven motivation of the plot.

Remember [[538-bioshock-infinite|BioShock Infinite]]? It's a great example of how the story that was all about cooperation and walking through all the chaos together suddenly throws cold water on the player by splitting the characters for some time. Booker wanders alone, seeking her, developing the narrative of "she's just a mission" versus "who she actually is" further. What's with Leon and Claire? They neither have any sort of connection nor do they have any motivation building up besides what's there in the first minute of their first meeting.

#### Leon and Ada

Ada's character progression comes into two phases. The first one is just a deus ex machina. I don't personally remember a single time when Tyrant targeted anyone but us in the police station, despite the fact that Ada was there almost as much as we were, and I'm not even mentioning Claire yet. Whenever Leon is in genuine danger, guess who comes to rescue? Ada, at the parking lot, all the time. When it's time for her to tell the actual details, bandage Leon's wounds or even kiss him, that's when we know it's the phase two appearing.

I didn't suspect her of anything for a good half of the game, as it was the game making at least some attempts at giving out the lore. Nevertheless, her manipulations were actually written pretty well. She actually didn't make much of a fuss over trying to make up the FBI backstory, and she was telling the truth regarding the G-Virus itself, so we definitely give her that. Her active, deliberate and concrete manipulation happened with some simple kiss, which in my opinion is very realistic and grounded, given Leon's immaturity.

It's actually the finest part of the character revelation of the entire game. Both Claire and Ada serve as the objects to depict Leon's immaturity. It's literally his first day as a cop. He thinks of law and order, rules and justice. He mentions his motivation to become a cop is wanting to help people and uphold law, which obviously sounds somewhat naive even if there's some underlying story regarding his actual motivation I'm not familiar with.

So it's no wonder he offers some trust in Ada and throws out pretentious phrases to Claire, sounding like a knight in shining armor in a classic romantic-adventure book. I find it rather funny to hear his "I'll stop this, lieutenant, I promise", when he literally knows naught about what's revolving around him. This is probably the best indirect characterization in the story so far, as it's subtle and noticeable, but still follows the rule "show, don't tell". Well, he tells, but you know what I mean.

### Claire's Part of the Story

At the time when Leon's part of the story came to an end with a very, and I mean incredibly, frustratingly short amount of time invested into showing what's happening in the end and what actually made William do all of that, it's already time to... do a second run. Sounds [[11208-nier-automata|very familiar]], huh?

And this is where it gets completely ridiculous. Her part has two noticeable features and both of them made me laugh harder than I ever imagined this game could make me laugh.

The first one is... um... well, the dreariness of repetition. I'm not going to repeat all of my thoughts on how RPD's gameplay cycle is debatable, but Claire's run feels genuinely insane. The mysteries of the ex-museum were **never** actually much engaging on their own. They follow the same repetitive cycle of "find the key, open the door", "find the safe, find the note, open the safe" and so on. They are all lock-and-key variations of the very same problem. And instead of making a brand new chapter of the story for Claire, Capcom decided it's going to be very interesting to force the player to roam the same corridors. Again!

There are a few new rooms with a nice design, and they feel great, I'm not gonna lie, but even if we speedrun through the rooms and secrets, it's still an hour or two at best for an average player. Finding medallions, killing the very same William multiple times, grabbing the very same bags and shells. This is simply **not** engaging by any means, unless you grab an infinite ammo rocket launcher and go blow up the entire station. The sewers? Yeah, they share the very same problem, as we still need to find the chess plugs just like we did with Leon. Umbrella's labs aren't better at all. The very same presence of Tyrant - if not more - makes the run even more hilarious in the worst way possible, despite all the new gun diversity and - let's be honest - Claire's nice appearance. Even little stuff like Leon's hints and Claire's phrases like "this doesn't sound like Chris" doesn't help much, although it's pretty pleasant to see.

But if the RPDs hellish repetition doesn't make sense to me at all, the sewers have a difference that did make me laugh so hard. It's the second noticeable feature of the run - Sherry. And don't get me wrong, as her presence in the game is probably the best thing that ever happened to RE 2. The orphanage's hiding quest is very intense and immersive. It shares the same core as Tyrant, but it **is** actually justified by Sherry's weak body and the context and it's not annoying and doesn't last long. The developers really showed that this concept could be done well.

What makes it even more ironic, it's not the last time they showed the writers are actually capable of writing a good story. A lot more cutscenes. Annette's "goodbye" to Sherry, Claire's "you're her mother, get in here!", the lucky jacket, a deeper understanding of what happened to William, inability of Annette to kill her husband right away, her own death, and finally... a decent happy ending for the story. This is SO good compared to what we've seen when playing as Leon.

This is why I find it hilarious to this minute. They deliberately decided to make most of the story of the Leon's part devastatingly hollow, while making actual good drama and lore when playing as Claire. Leon's run lacks emotional stakes, while Claire's run is both rich in characters and the plot. But I can't see a motivation for an average player to run this game for the second time and roam the same corridors again, waiting for something new - the orphanage - to appear before their eyes. There's just no sense. Moreover, I talk about the repetition so much it feels like I'm repeating myself as well, isn't it ironic?

For that matter, the other thing that makes the "two runs" structure feel very odd is that they share the same building and timeline, while offering almost no interactions between the main characters. They are literally in alternate realities most of the time, never crossing their paths in the RPD and the sewers. This hurts the immersion enormously while also offering nothing good in terms of the gameplay. Again, [[11208-nier-automata|NieR: Automata]] had the very same issue with that, force-feeding players a repetitive gameplay for the sake of the few new cutscenes and chests. This is the same logic and the same flaw. But NieR had at least some sort of explanation of cycles and thus these re-runs. Leon's and Claire's runs inconsistency and lack of intercept couldn't be justified using any piece of lore. Thank God the orphanage is actually decent and pretty long.

### Conclusion

As it turns out, the story kicks off with a blockbuster premise, where Leon basks in glory, shooting the zombie behind Claire's back even when the player left him with no ammo during the tutorial, but that very same story doesn't develop in Leon's run at all. Claire is somewhere there and we don't think about her the slightest bit, losing such a big chunk of possible motivation and deeper genre diversity just to be able to wander around zombies and corridors with more quiet and less... well, passion in playing the game. It's not a narrative decision, it's a dirty gameplay hack. We play to save the city, to kill the zombies, to get the hell outta there, whatever, but is it really a good motivation for the player, not Leon?

Leon's ending itself is very controversial. It's designed in a way that feels like the authors were absolutely invested in the gameplay itself, while the story is just a small addition to the endless corridor roaming. He just runs to the train, admits he misses Ada despite everything, and meets with Claire. For a brief moment yet again, uh-huh. And this could be seen as not blatantly outrageous, but the thing is based on what we've seen so far in the aforementioned gameplay notes, this game can provide **neither** of these on a really non-groaning level. It doesn't feature a good gameplay or a decent storyline, instead relying solely on a player's imagination, incredibly immersive sound and visual design, and basic survival horror elements like ammo and heal economy.

When we are playing as Claire, we are finally able to witness this anthropocentric narrative and a genuinely good motivation to proceed with the game even when it's offering same locations and mysteries. But isn't it a little too late? It's a very good experience, indeed, down to every goosebump on my skin, when the actual Annette-Sherry-Claire story takes place, but it's very odd to see it after investing so much time in a survival horror that became tedious as hell.

You don't have to play the original game to understand this one, as the city is right before your eyes and there are many documents, cassettes and cutscenes explaining briefly what happened. But if you aren't invested in the series already, then I can see no reasons for it to capture your attention here and now for the good half of the game. Yes, the zombies have very grounded physics and sounds, and the storm outside the windows is very subtle in a good way. And yes, the visuals are great, and the environmental storytelling is unquestionably there, in Leon's locked desk ("Welcome, Leon" party decorations etc) and on the lab's whiteboards and so on, but it's not enough. You have to tolerate game's unjustified conventions for many hours before actually seeing a catharsis you weren't even expecting here to be at this point.

And at the end of the day, the story is actually good. But the game could've been a hundred times better both in terms of the gameplay and the story. Therefore, we have a decent resource-economy-driven anti-shooter with good physics and attempts at horror, which then crashes by putting just too many tedious decisions into the gameplay and almost nothing to the story until the orphanage part, while also revealing there **is** a genuinely good story, but they just don't invest time into that mostly. The best I can hope is that the other games featuring Leon would depict his fully-fledged character development in true colors.
